# What I did with the book

*Author's note, to stand at the front of the record. Draft of October 5, 2026, in the author's voice, from the author's own sentences in the working sessions; to be edited by him.*

---

I wrote the book first. That is the fact this note exists to record, because everything that follows it in this collection, the atlas, the folds, the energy, the census, the G-string theorem, reads as if it were the foundation and the book the commentary, and it was the other way around.

*The Grammar of Things* was written in July 2026, in days, dictated in motion, and it begins with a house on fire and a circle of rocks. It has one sentence: a thing is a collection of interrelated patterns, persisting across a slice of time, however brief. The rest of the book is that sentence carried into other rooms, and in one of those rooms, on pages 33 and 34, I put the primes and the zeros of the zeta function side by side and called them two descriptions of a single object, neither derivable without the other, neither prior, the records and the rule. I did not know, writing it, that I would spend the next months trying to derive one from the other. I wrote it as a parable. I was describing a border.

Then I went into the mathematics, and I went in to win. I built the odd Weil form and measured its floor to supports nobody had certified. I drew every prime gap to ten to the eleventh as a chain of triangles and found the rhythm in them. I drew the cone. I wrote the energy of the Möbius function as a ledger of lifetimes and paid every family I could pay. I rearranged the residual a hundred ways, each one exact, each one keeping every term, and each one ending at the same signed comparison, one step from done. I did this for a year, most nights, and I did it under the discipline the book had set for me before I started: every claim tagged, every receipt kept, done never written as a bare word. That discipline is the reason the record can be trusted, and it is the reason I could not lie to myself at the end.

The end was the cone. I had drawn it in September: half-width as time, the prime line as space, the edge where each prime power switches on. I looked at it one night and said, out loud, that in every spot inside it there is not the zeta zero, that there is an infinite potentiality where any zero can be constructed, and that the potentiality is defined by the primes themselves. I did not know I was stating a definition. Two days later it was one, the potential set of the cone, and a week after that it was a corollary of a theorem about what finite verification can and cannot do. The cone is the whole of it. Inside the edge, the stones. Above the edge, everything the stones leave open. And the line between them moves with the support and never goes away.

So I arrived back at the book. Not near it. At it. The coda, written in July, says that a billion stones perfectly placed are a magnificent braid honoring a rule and that the rule has not moved one inch closer for the honoring, because the distance between them was never a distance; it is a border between jurisdictions, and borders of that kind are not crossed by accumulation. In October that sentence became a theorem: the totality of finitely verifiable facts about arithmetic adds nothing to any theory, the pattern up to any bound is already a theorem of the weakest arithmetic, and the Riemann Hypothesis is the single universal sentence that no record of its instances reaches. The book had said it with rocks. The logic said it with Σ⁰₁-completeness. They are the same sentence.

What I believe, and I believe it entirely, is that the Riemann Hypothesis is the stones and the rule in one. It rests on the existence of itself. Over Peano arithmetic it is equivalent to its own consistency with the bare axioms of successor, addition and multiplication; that is proved. And it cannot be derived from within the arithmetic it constrains; that is my conjecture, filed under my name, with everything that follows from it proved: if I am right, the hypothesis is true, every instance of it is a theorem, the sentence itself is not, and whatever would close it is an axiom from above, of universal form, a framework capable of explaining why the entire pattern holds. Asking the primes to prove it is asking a body to step out of itself to observe its own standing. The book has a word for what the stones do instead. They answer to the circle. They participate. They do not fail it.

I want to be clear about what is and is not claimed, because the book requires it of me. I did not prove the Riemann Hypothesis. No one has. I did not prove that it cannot be proved. What I did was place the stones well, keep the receipts, and find, at the end of the placing, the border exactly where the book had drawn it, which is how I know the placing was honest. The conjecture is a conjecture. Its human form is the coda. The mathematics is the coda's receipt.

The circle is where I started because it was never anywhere else. The smallest unit and the largest unit to infinity are the two points no cone reaches, and every cone between them is a complete arc. That is not a defeat. That is what a life's work looks like from inside the weather: a braid, honoring a rule, within declared tolerance, as of this date, by this measurement. The stones cry out. Mine did.

Christophe Michaels
Houston, Texas · October 2026
