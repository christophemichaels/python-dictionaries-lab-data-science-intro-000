# The Grammar of Things: PDF description

## Short (for a post or a listing)

The Grammar of Things. Eight volumes, in two canons, with a preface and a coda. 173 pages. Christophe Michaels, Houston, July 2026.

The world is not furniture. The world is weather that holds its shapes. From that finding, and one sentence, a thing is a collection of interrelated patterns persisting across a slice of time, however brief, the book rebuilds ontology, reads the Christian scriptures, defines knowing as a ledger rather than a warehouse, locates most of human goodness in the tending, grades every economic system as bookkeeping, and then turns inward to the workplace, the unrecorded self, and the uses and limits of cunning before power. It ends with a coda on a circle of stones that never becomes the circle, and on why that is not a tragedy. Every claim carries its receipt; every unresolved question is kept open, dated, with its reasons.

## Full (back cover)

The Grammar of Things is a complete philosophical system worked out in the margins of a working life, in plain declarative English, and offered with its receipts.

It begins with a house on fire and a word that would not let go: for days after the roof fell, house went on pointing at rubble. From that scene the first volume rebuilds what a thing is. Not a substance, not a snapshot, but a collection of interrelated patterns persisting across a slice of time, however brief. Identity is a lineage. A name is a debt, a pointer that promises continuity with a referent it can never quite touch. Rules, the triangle, the theorem, the number, are not things at all but timeless constraints that things answer to; and at the edge of a world of rules and braids stands an office the tradition has always had a word for, derived here with care and left, honestly, with its deepest question filed as unresolvable from inside.

Four more volumes carry the grammar into the oldest scriptures, into knowing, into goodness, and into the marketplace and the state: the tenseless name at the burning bush; forgiveness as a ledger that freezes the event and versions its authority; knowledge as a claim held with its thread and its window, under audit; harm as the unraveling of coherence and love as commitment to a lineage rather than any slice of it; money as the purest name ever minted and every economy as a common ledger of promises, good exactly insofar as its books are complete and its keepers can be checked.

Then the system turns and asks what it is worth on a Monday. A second canon, psychological and applied, written as cases first and law after, takes the grammar into the three rooms where a life is spent: the workplace as a lattice of promises, where nearly every misery is a bookkeeping failure before it is a people failure; the dark ledger of the self, where what is not recorded under its true name does not stop running; and the serpent's constitution, which separates forging the record, forbidden absolutely, from governing the interface, licensed narrowly, upward, at one's own cost.

A coda closes the whole shelf with a question the first yard left sleeping. Place a thousand stones, ground flush and surveyed by laser, and has the arrangement become the circle? It has not, and it never will. The gap between a braid and the rule it honors is not a distance that effort closes but a border between jurisdictions, and borders of that kind are not crossed by accumulation. The stones do not fail the circle. They answer to it.

The book does not prove the existence of God, and says so. It does not settle the contested edges of the moral life. It does not ask to be believed. It asks to be audited.
